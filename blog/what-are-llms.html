What are Large Language Models (LLMs)?

October 17, 2026 · 18 min read

On my last blog post, I talked about AI as a broad term, what it represents in everybody's mind and what it really is in the inside.

Today, I want to correlate AI, Large Language Models (LLM), Machine Learning (ML), Deep Learning (DL), Neural Networks (NN) and other big words to give you all a better understanding of each concept and how they relate to each other.

Just a quick reminder: the opinions expressed here are stricly my own. They do not represent the opinions or views of my current employer nor any of my previous ones.

Context Is Key​

In 2019, lots of people came together and wrote Artificial Intelligence in the Management of Glioma: Era of Personalized Medicine in which they described "the relationship between the most common AI methods in medicine". Later, in 2023, Pan Singh Dhoni described "the relationship between AI, Machine Learning and Deep Learning" in his research titled Exploring the Synergy between Generative AI, Data and Analytics in the Modern Age.

Both described Artificial Intelligence as a set containing all kind of other subsets (in the mathematical sense of it), like Machine Learning, Deep Learning and Neural Network.

Thanks to complementary studies like Large language models: a primer and gastroenterology applications (2024) and Artificial Intelligence in Emergency Medicine: A Primer for the Nonexpert (2025), more terminology terms could be added to similar schemas, such as Generative AI (GenAI) and Large Language Models (LLM).

Taking into account all their work, we can draw the following AI Taxonomy which pretty much explains the relationship between each. I will use it as a base in this article.

Artificial Intelligence (AI)​

AI is not new, as stated in my previous blog post. At its core, AI really is just about enabling machines to perform tasks requiring a certain level of intelligence.

Machine Learning (ML)​

Zooming in, there is this big subset called Machine Learning.

In the late 80s, general belief was that building an "intelligent behaviour" could be achieved by explicitly writing down logical rules and knowledge (bunch of if-else stating how to act in certain conditions). One programming language that perfectly fits that conviction is Prolog: a declarative programming language based on a set of facts, rules and relations and where a computation takes the form of a query.

However, the limitations are increasingly evident: encoding knowledge by hand is labor-intensive and an ever-ending task. This is where Machine Learning come in. As knowledge-based systems disappointed by their lack of generalization of knowledge, studies on more automated learning techniques started to emerge, especially thanks to new algorithms development like Bayesian inference and Iterative Dichotomiser 3. Instead of experts providing domain rules, we provide labeled data and let the algorithm infer the rules (training phase). This inverted the knowledge acquisition problem! Data-driven AI was born.

Machine Learning systems are thus systems fed with data that use algorithms to recognize patterns and infer rules from it. Its most important phase is the "learning" part, where the algorithms would improve their performance on a task through data: the more data, the better output accuracy in the end.

Neural Networks (NN)​

While studies on ML systems were going great, other forms of AI computation were researched to fulfill gaps in many domains like vision or natural language understanding, as those tasks were proved hard to define or infer rules from dataset.

Indeed, to train an ML system, you'd have to go through the dataset to label the information. In the end, you still depend on humans selecting useful features to train the system (e.g. to recognize a cat in an image, you might require manually designed features representing paws, whiskers, ears and so on...).

Artificial Neural Network (ANN)​

Researchers tried to teach the systems pattern recognition from the training data. They mimicked the human brain's model for information processing and created Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs).

An artificial neural network is a computational model composed of interconnected artificial neurons (typically organized in layers) that transforms inputs into outputs. An artificial neuron is actually a highly simplified mathematical abstraction that combines several input values with adjustable weights, adds a bias and applies an activation function.

If you read that out loud, it does not even help to understand it, right? Well, let me give you an example to simplify it. Imagine that we want to predict whether a student will pass an exam based on three factors:

  1. hours studied (how long the student prepared the exam)
  2. hours slept (how much sleep did they get the night before)
  3. attendance (the percentage of classes attended)

Instead of manually defining rules, we train an ANN to learn the relationships between these factors and the exam results. To achieve this, we would give it historical data as training dataset:

In order to train the system to learn the patterns and to predict the output value, the ANN will repeatedly:

  • predict whether a student will pass based on actual input values
  • compare its prediction with the actual result
  • calculate the error between them
  • adjust its weights using backpropagation and an optimization algorithm
  • repeat the process across many examples

Over time, it learns useful relationships between study habits, attendance, sleep, and obviously the exam results.

What's impressive is not simply predicting the outcome (especially in this context where the formula is quite simple), it's more about how the prediction is learned! An ANN learns numerical weights and combinations of inputs from examples. Using backpropagation, the hidden neurons unlock much richer learning behaviours and thus could discover useful features and encodings of the input domain rather than relying on manually written human-designed rules.

At the time they got developed, however, neural networks were not that fabulous... mainly due to the limits of computational power and the size of available data. Early neural networks established a general principle though: interconnected mathematical units can learn from data. And different tasks required different structures!

  • Images benefit from networks that recognize local spatial patterns.
  • Complex abstractions benefit from networks with many processing layers.
  • Sequences benefit from networks that maintain information across time.

These requirements led to Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), Deep Neural Networks (DNNs), and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs).

Deep Learning (DL)​

DNN and Deep Learning are closely related terms: the first is a model architecture for neural networks based on multiple layers of learned representations to model complex patterns in data ; the second refers to a broader approach of training and using such models.

The power of neural networks comes from scaling them up in depth, width and data, making deep neural networks a go-to approach in AI research: it was setting new records in different tasks such as "image classification, object detection, machine translation, language modelling" (R. Mundlamuri, G. R. Gunnam, N. K. Mysari and J. Pujuri, 2025).

Transformers​

Deep learning significantly improved perception and language processing, but recurrent architectures (RNNs) had important limitations: they process sequences through recurrent steps, making large-scale parallel training difficult. They can also struggle with very long-range dependencies.

For example, imagine a deep RNN reads a story and stumbles across this sentence: The dog chase the ball because it was red. What does the "it" means? The dog? The ball? As a human, you immediately understand that "it" refers to the ball, because balls can be red and the sentence makes sense that way. But how does the computer do that?

To fix this kind of issues, researchers developed attention mechanisms so models could focus selectively on relevant parts of an input. This led to the Transformer architecture.

Older neural networks, like RNNs, process words one after another, passing information along as they go. A Transformer does something different: it allows every word to look at other words and decide which ones matter most. This process is called "self-attention": it helps the transformer calculates how strongly different words should influence one another.

Transformers are thus deep neural network architectures built around attention mechanisms, especially self-attention. They replace recurrent processing with attention-based computation, making training more parallelizable.

Generative AI (GenAI)​

Up to now, we talked about a lot of systems that are able to predict an output value based on input values. These are what we call classifiers, they are used to predict a category based on the values received as input.

GenAI refers to AI systems that also learn patterns or distributions from data but they use them to generate new content such as text, image, audio, video, code and structured information. Unlike classifiers, a generative model produces an output resembling or satisfying patterns learned during training.

A few years ago, when you asked Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini or DeepSeek for a random number between 1 and 20, they used to answer 17. How is that random if you ask AI systems and they all give you the same number? (J. Agrawal, 2025).

As generative models predicts what the next word will be, based on their training data, it only takes data where more representations of "generate a random number between 1 and 20" gives "17" to lead to a biased generative model. This is a part of the explanation as to why some AI systems generated contents that were deemed incorrect or inappropriate at times: bias.

However, GenAI are not the only systems that are biased: anything that infers patterns from data will perpetuate reality biases through the learned representations (e.g. classifiers like Microsoft facial recognition services, which had issues identifying women and dark-skinned individuals (Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018)). This is inherent to model training and cannot be avoided. However, it can be mitigated. And mitigation is also a tough job!

Did you know that when Google first released Gemini, they said they designed it as an attempt to avoid perpetuating pre-existing societal prejudices? But in fact, they actually overcompensated the bias, and ended up leaning toward another issue of misrepresentation of "mainstream culture" (Shamim, 2024).

In the end, what are Large Language Models (LLMs) ? Well, it's rather simple: LLMs are a subset of GenAI that are specialized in text generation. Just like MidJourney is a generative AI specialized in image generation, and MusicGen is a generative AI specialized in audio generation, LLMs such as GPT, Gemini, Claude, Mistral or DeepSeek are generative AI specialized in text.

Why is it called like that though?

  • Large: it has a very large number of learned parameters, sometimes billions or more. These numerical values help encode patterns learned during training.
  • Language: it is trained to process language: words, sentences, conversations, and other text-based information, including programming languages.
  • Model: it is a mathematical system that learns statistical patterns and uses them to make predictions.

Thus, an LLM is a deep-learning system built on the Transformer Architecture, trained on massive text datasets to understand, generate, and reason about human language. It learns by predicting the next token in a sequence repeated trillions of times across terabytes of data.

What are LLMs' limitations, evolution and future through model iterations ?​

In the past years, LLMs have evolved from relatively simple text-prediction systems into models capable of writing softwares, analyzing documents, solving mathematical problems, processing images and interacting with external tools (via "connectors").

However, being able to generate convincing answers is not the same as

  1. being accurate,
  2. being reliable, and
  3. being capable of understanding every situation.

Evolution​

LLMs will undoubtedly continue to grow and become more performant. We already witness significant improvements with each new model iteration, from better language understanding to enhanced reasoning capabilities.

Hence even developers say things like "the AI writes better code than us".

What drives their evolution is:

  • scale (by increasing the amount of training data, parameters and computational power) ;
  • capabilities (by creating more connectors to move beyond text generation (e.g. multimodality, extensive reasoning, etc.)) ;
  • reliability (by improving factual accuracy, verification and efficiency - especially in code generation).

Limitations​

You might already have used AI and you already found out about a few of its limitations. You can find a non-exhaustive list in the table below.

Limited knowledge freshnessTraining knowledge can become outdatedDescribing an obsolete software API as current
Context limitationsModels can miss, forget, or misinterpret information in long conversations or documentsOverlooking a requirement in a 200-page specification

Future​

The future of LLMs and the frontier models in a generic point of view may be the transition from models that generate answers to systems that can plan, act, check their work and collaborate with people.

This includes:

  • native multimodal handling (integrate text, images, audio, video and potentially sensor information at some point to enrich the LLMs representations of the world - part of this is already the case) ;
  • more deliberate reasoning (extract computation allocated for difficult tasks, exploring alternative solutions, checking intermediate results, etc.) ;
  • more capable agents (instead of explaining how to perform a task, it could carry out multi-step workflows using tools, with checkpoints, permissions and human supervision - we already see that in the IT sphere where developers are now becoming AI-produced code reviewers... but this will be for another article as well!) ;
  • better factual verification (rather than producing a plausible answer, they can fact-check it) ;
  • better long-term context and memory (persist information more effectively, maintain coherence across lengthy conversations, etc.).

What you see coming for the future of LLMs is a shift towards more integrated, capable, and reliable systems that can interact with the world in a more human-like manner, reason more effectively, and maintain long-term memory of specific usecases.

Conclusion​

In the end, we talked about the whole history behind LLMs and what they really are. We also mentioned their limits, their evolution and what we can see coming for the future.
